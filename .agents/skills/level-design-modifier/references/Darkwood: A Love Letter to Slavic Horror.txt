And let's come back to the Hard Mode we talked about in the beginning the one where you only get a couple of lives and then your save file is erased. There’s something the devs purposefully didn’t tell you about. The fact that your game gets erased is only true for the first area of the game. But you don’t know that. In the early hours of the game, when you are still the fully human, defiant intruding force, your game *will* be deleted once you die one too many times. But once you go deeper into the woods, the game becomes more and more forgiving the further you go, because unlike it initially claimed, in later parts in the game, it doesn’t even erase your save file anymore but will only revert to the beginning of the current chapter. Even on hard mode - it slowly eases the thumbscrews when it comes to the innate value of death, and how you, as the player, perceive it. Because just like the trader, who has long forgotten or... completely lost? his ability to converse with human language, who’s by now impossible to differentiate from the uncontrollable organic mass that claims the entirety of the zone; in the same way *you* are turning more and more into a part of the forest yourself. To go full circle about morality -- you remember the kick-the-dog moment from earlier, when we still played as the doctor in the prologue? We had the unprompted choice to either kill it or leave it alive. We eventually return to the doctor’s place later in the game, and depending on your choice from earlier -- the dog will either be resting in peace, or it will have metanmorphosed into one of the gnarly zone dogs that jumps you out of nowhere and tries to tear your throat out. The doctor is, in many ways, the ultimate test for the player’s moral compass and how much of the game’s almost – buddhist, "we’re all part of one eclectic organism"-reality the player has taken to heart at this point. Because when you eventually meet him, there is pretty much not a single thing in this game that pointed towards him not a “Truly Horrible Person” in this tale. He acted like a vile piece of shit in the prologue, seemingly treated his dog like a piece of shit, and then treated you like a piece of shit when he found you unconscious and immediately went on to torture you for information. When you finally meet him -- and it takes quite a bit of grit and perseverance to get there nothing keeps you from just killing him right there on the spot, and grab the key he stole from you and escape. And - yeah, it’s a completely valid choice in this game, nothing prevents you from it, and nothing will even condemn you for it afterwards either. But if you show remorse, and spare him, and give in to his pleas -- you will find that even this seemingly clear cut judge of character is very much a matter of perspective and motive. Because as it turns out eventually -- if it weren't for him, you actually wouldn’t have been alive anymore anymore at all. At least not in the .. traditional sense. Many times throughout the game, you get these warnings: Don’t go deeper, because the woods will claim you; they will change you. And those warnings, all of them – are absolutely true. But you sincerely start questioning if this is actually a bad thing. Almost reliably, the more a character you meet opposes and defies the woods, the more... they suffer. In a very dark and melancholy way, Darkwood’s at first glance, incredibly harsh and unforgiving world suggests that entropy is the natural way of life, and that defying it is suffering. If you give in to the eldritch allure, the woods unquestionably welcome you as an integral part of it. It’s, as I said, surprisingly buddhist. We are all connected – it’s only a sense of scale and scope. And that what anchors you to your individuality is suffering. But we’re all part of something much grander than ourselves. Something that feels inscrutably and terrifyingly indifferent, yet omnipotently welcoming at the same time. Darkwood’s world almost ridicules the transient nature of humanity in their fight against entropy; the things we create -reclaimed by the divine mercilessness of an entity that is far greater, older, wiser and more powerful than anything people can conceive. We are, and have always been part of it. No matter how much we reject it. It is poignantly ironic that by the time we finally escape the woods, we are not the same person anymore as when we began this odyssey. Such is the nature of The Zone. It is a place that changes you for good. Once we return to the Krushchyovka we used to call our home, the elusive goal that we’ve carried around on a withered photograph for the entirety of the game until we’ve nearly forgotten about it, it feels like ... we don’t even belong here anymore. It feels like once we’re back in the promised safety of civilization we’ve longed for, we are now as much a foreign body within its cold and grey prison walls of concrete in the same way as we’ve started out as a foreign body invading the sanctity of the organism of the forest when we started out. The zone and the trek deeper and deeper into its heart is a journey that inexorably changes you; it evolves you. A transition that’s not convenient, nor sedate or comfortable – but it is a destination that, once you’ve reached it, you’ll never be able nor willing to go back to how it was before. The zone embraces us and spits us out as something pure, something so fundamentally altered, so much closer to an unfiltered, unadultered core of our true selves, that there can’t be any place on earth left that we belong in anymore after we've been through it. Until the only sane choice you’re left with is to burn it all to the ground... burn... them... all.....
