The kind of terror present in Amnesia: The Bunker isn’t unique. That doesn’t mean it’s not effective. More than a decade ago, the first Amnesia game popularized a trend that would take over mid-budget horror: helplessness. No guns, no killable enemies, no way to fight back. Unlike Resident Evils or Silent Hills, if you saw a monster in Amnesia, the only recourse was to flee to the nearest closet or under the bed and wait there, vision swimming with encroaching insanity, as the all-powerful monster stalked around. It was- it is- deeply scary. Total un-empowerment, the childhood horror of a boogeyman that couldn’t be stopped by anything. It’s also a bit of a one-trick pony. In Amnesia’s sequels and the other games it inspired, the invincible boogeymen straddled the line between scare and chore. I would notice a closet and think “great, that’s where I’ll run to when a bad guy shows up in 45 seconds.” Even brilliant titles like SOMA suffered from the tedium of waiting around for bad and impervious robot-men to wander in a different direction. And so finally, thirteen years after The Dark Descent, the Amnesia series has made a startling choice: it’s giving you back your gun. The new Amnesia game seemingly reverses course on its entire identity– revolvers, grenades, molotovs, all will fill your pockets over the course of the game. And in doing so, it finds a path between the helplessness of earlier titles and the walking arsenals of Residents Evil. Amnesia: The Bunker is not simply a game of hide and seek. Instead, with its new mechanics, it cultivates a very specific kind of terror, one in which the apparent gift of agency is punished with self-imposed paralysis of choice. It’s a feeling that lives in a particular part of your brain. But, in the intricacies of our psyche, we’re capable of feeling dread, fear, distress, in near-infinite ways. It’s this…gift…of our evolution that allows The Bunker to operate on a completely different level than, say, a game in which your face is only slightly yours to control, or one in which you’re swallowed under the shadow of a being many miles high. But, we’ll get there. After we get through The Bunker. It’s fairly easy to intuit the setting of the game from its title. Situated within an active no-man’s land during World War 1, 95% of The Bunker takes place in, you know, a hole in the ground. It’s a tremendously evocative period to set a horror game; the nightmarish war serving as the easy real-life analogue to whatever supernatural scary thing the writers want to happen at the same time. The game’s mechanical inspirations are less obvious, however. That is, until you start looking at how it approaches the classic horror game trope of The Locked Door. It’s hard to name a horror game that doesn’t deal in some way with locked doors. They could necessitate a multi-piece puzzle like in Resident Evil, or the futile rattling of endless broken doorknobs a la Silent Hill, finding keycards in Signalis, smashing power conduits in Dead Space, the list goes on. But almost always, the locked door is a separate challenge from the game’s other systems; in one half of our brain we carry the worry of a locked door, and in the other half we juggle our remaining resources for fighting off enemies. The Bunker takes the deceptively simple step of removing this separation. After handing you a revolver, the first thing The Bunker asks you to shoot is not a monster or enemy combatant, but a padlock. It’s almost cathartic. No searching pockets or corners for a lost key! Just pull the hammer back and blam, lock gone, door open. But it shouldn’t take long for that catharsis to fade into an omnipresent anxiety. Because there is a monster in The Bunker. Of course there is. And with rarely enough ammunition to even fill the cylinder of your revolver, every padlock blasted open becomes one fewer bullet you have to deter a charging beast, and every shot at that beast becomes one more door you’ll potentially never unlock. But it’s not just a binary. Wooden doors are flimsy enough that they’ll crumble after hurling a cinder block at them a few times, but that cinder block will echo down the halls, announcing your exact position to anyone who might be listening. Grenades can serve the same purpose but are even louder, and is a door really worth giving up the security a grenade provides? As several other writers have pointed out, The Bunker owes much of its mechanical flair to the immersive sim genre, games like Deus Ex or Prey with many solutions to one problem. But unlike those games, the excitement of finding a way through in The Bunker is almost always paired with the pain of burning one of your lifelines. The horror-logic of those first Amnesia games was fairly straightforward: without any way to fight back, every monster poses the ultimate threat, and thus every encounter gains the highest possible stakes. But The Bunker rolls with perhaps an even more human rationality: decision is agonizing. Even the most optimal strategy is going to have trade offs here, and benefiting somewhere inevitably means weakening yourself somewhere else. And alongside its new resource anxiety, this mechanical shift unexpectedly addresses Amnesia’s other biggest problem: death. Death has always been a sticking point for the helpless-horror genre. On one hand, the monsters need to pose some danger to the player’s health or else there’s no threat. On the other, dying kinda cuts all the tension of the level. Reaching a game-over screen in one of those titles only serves to remind us “oh yeah, death is meaningless in a video game, I’ll just go back to the checkpoint.” Early on, this seemed unchanged in The Bunker. Actually, the problem seemed compounded! Miss one of my 3 shots with the revolver? I’ll just die, rather than live with the consequences of that missed shot. But The Bunker doesn’t work on an exploitable save system- it has exactly one manual save point, right in the middle of the map, and doesn’t offer multiple save slots either. So as you explore the deeper parts of the map, venturing further and further from the one save slot, finding more resources and story-focused journals and uncovering new objectives, the consequences of dying and losing everything you’ve found start to outweigh the consequences of missing a shot. You have more to lose, so you have more to protect. Deep in the bowels of the earth, I used my precious resources with panicked abandon, shooting at ghosts and hurling grenades at rodents, unsure if I’d even be able to find my way back here if I died. Oh, and there’s the issue of the generator. See, the bunker does actually have electricity. It is…imperfect, at best, dim and flickering and prone to make a hallway look absolutely terrifying. But it’s still vastly preferable to your other light sources, a crank-powered flashlight or quick-burning torches and flares. All of the electricity in the bunker comes from a gas-powered generator that sits right next to the one save point, and the gas sits in one-time use cans scattered around the bunker. One can will buy you about 5 minutes of light. So while it may be tempting to scurry back to the save room every time you make any sort of progress, these sorts of return trips will actually burn away what few minutes of light you have. If you play too cautiously, you will straight up run out of fuel, even the most familiar twistsof the bunker turning hostile in the black. I dreaded these situations so much that I would use any resource to avoid them, even if I missed my shot, even if it was the last bullet in my chamber. The Bunker’s atmosphere is so relentlessly oppressive that it’s able to pull off an impressively cruel trick. Hours into the game, you climb up a ladder and find yourself faced with something unexpected: the sky. Out of the slot window, the sun paints the horizon orange and blue, an environmental warmth absent from anywhere in the tunnels. It’s so close, and the window isn’t that narrow…maybe, I thought, and then a rifle cracked and the concrete above my head exploded. I more or less threw myself back into the tunnels. It’s World War 1 out there, remember? There is little present in The Bunker that feels wholly unique– I have seen loud and persistent monsters before, I have wandered through the dark, I have worried about my ammo. What’s notable here is how dreadfully tight it’s turned the screws, that each of its impressively fleshed out systems ties back into a core of anxiety about something, about everything, running out. Not enough fuel, not enough bullets, not enough light to make it through the next hour. It’s the kind of experience particularly suited to this medium, your ability to make choices becoming the very thing that might do you in. But at least they are, like…your choices. “Who’s Lila” was released last year by developer Garage Heathen. It’s kinda a dialogue based point-and-click adventure game, except with the notable difference that you’re not actually able to pick dialogue. Instead, as the protagonist informs you in the opening moments of the game, “it’s difficult for me to express emotions. I envy other people. They make faces naturally. But I have to make a conscious decision each time I move a muscle.” Rather than choosing from a dialogue tree, Who’s Lila gives you the human face as a canvas to paint your emotional reaction to things upon. As it happens, you are probably a very bad painter. Before setting out into the world, the protagonist invites you to practice your faces in the mirror. He thinks “when I see my neighbor, she’ll ask how I am and I will answer good, thank you.” On the other half of the screen, sits a grainy, expressionless portrait. You grab the corners of his mouth and pull, stretching each side individually into an uneasy smile. “If some weird guy approaches me at the store, I’ll scare him away” and you pull his lips into a grimace and his eyebrows into a scowl. “If I once again see the one hiding in boilers” he thinks, and we sculpt his face like uncooperative clay into some version of “scared.” There are no resources to manage in Who’s Lila, no dwindling pool of generator fuel or single bullets to juggle. Instead, the game can live for a little while on just how f**king weird this mechanic is, twisting your character’s face like taffy into a poor imitation of “excited” or “sad.” It is, in effect, a horror game made out of the start screen of Mario 64. Aside from the inherent uncanniness though, this central mechanic is almost begging you to try out completely inappropriate responses to people’s questions. Who’s Lila centers around the disappearance of one of the protagonist’s classmates, a woman named Tanya Kennedy. Often, you’ll find yourself questioned by people about your interactions with Tanya, if you know anything about her, why you were spending so much time with her before she disappeared. Almost always, the “correct” response to their queries is to look sad, surprised, or even just stay neutral. But what if, huh? What if you broke into a big shit-eating grin when a classmate says that no one has heard from her in a week? Sometimes, the game has this idea for you. At the beginning, our character- a kid named Will, according to his classmates- said that he has trouble expressing emotions, that he has to make a conscious decision to move every muscle. But that doesn’t actually seem true. Often, when confronted or surprised with information, Will’s face starts to move on its own. The corners of his mouth twitch, his eyes widen or narrow into exactly the opposite of what you’d hope. You don’t just mold his expression in these scenes, you actively fight what it seems like he’s trying to do. You wrestle over control of his body. When I was a kid, I had this weird recurring nightmare where I’d get in trouble for something I hadn’t done at school. I’d get called to the principal’s office and they’d ask me point-blank, “did you do this?” And the answer would be no, and I’d try to answer no. But despite not having done it, I wouldn’t be able to stop myself from grinning or giggling in an obviously guilty way. Inevitably, the principal would decide I was responsible. It was one of those weird, low-stakes nightmares that nevertheless popped up for years. There’s this pervasive question of who, or what, is really in control in Who’s Lila. Usually, Will’s face doesn’t move at all without our input– but sometimes it moves on its own. Usually, his locomotion is determined by pointing and clicking on a place on the screen– but sometimes, the game cuts to first person. Usually, we’re only asked to manipulate his expression in conversation, but sometimes– [SHUT YOUR EYES]. Progressing through multiple playthroughs of the game expands its possibility space. Early on, you can only take the bus to school. Eventually, you can ride it to several different locations in town, including a burned out building with a symbol that…hypnotizes you, I guess, into an entirely different space, into one of those first person sections, and a horrid morphing figure eventually gives you…a link. Like a web link, to a steam page. In a weirdly invasive moment, the figure and the game inform you that the link is “now in your clipboard,” and it…is. It’s there. And going to that link downloads a separate program to run on your computer simultaneously to Who’s Lila, and that program is just this bizarre demon face that sits on your desktop, and it also tells you what to do, croaking out numbers and advice in a little digitized voice. [LOOK AT THE DIARY TABLE] There’s only a few lines of spoken dialogue in the game. Some of it comes from the side-program you download. Some only consists of panting, or screams. And then there’s a phone call you get at the start of the game on your dozenth or so runthrough, a cell phone that says in english, “Lila please…I beg you, let me back in.” The game’s title, you probably noticed, asks a question. “Who’s Lila?” The easy answer is, it’s you. You are not William. You’re Lila. You’re the one who needs to adjust their facial expressions manually, you’re the one who lies to classmates and the cops, you’re the one who takes hints from demons. “Lila” is a presence that most are completely unaware of in the game, though sometimes they already know and sometimes your character will treat it as a sort of sadistic reveal. The lore of Lila runs deep, deep enough that even if you’ve watched this far, the game would still hold many surprises for you. But the general mythos of Lila is an entity that can take over others, not simply a possession but a forceful ejection of the prior “self” of that body. That’s the call we got earlier, that was William, the rightful owner of the body we’re now puppeteering around, confined to a voice that can barely whisper through a phone. And in this dispossession of the supposed protagonist, Who’s Lila adds another layer to its question of control. The expressions you’re smearing across William’s face don’t belong to him, but do they belong to you instead? How much of “Lila” is a literal, comprehensible being, and how much of “Lila” is just the anxiety of bodily autonomy, that the logical, rational signals we want to send to our face and body could be superseded by an entirely different set of impulses? It’s an intensely internal fear, presented through that primary mechanic. The pulling and stretching and pinching of flesh. William wouldn’t choose to make a face that looks like this. No one would. And yet… The final game I want to talk about today has no fuel generators, no failable dialogues, and no moving faces. It’s not interested in resource management or agency over flesh. It’s– you know what, actually I misspoke. The Utility Room, a VR title by micro developer Binman games, is very interested in moving faces. Just not in quite the same way. VR does a couple things very well. Physical manipulation of objects, rhythm games, the dark. But one of the things it’s best at is the motif tackled by The Utility Room: “what if things were really, really, really big.” The idea of The Utility Room as a location is a sort of behind-the-scenes for the universe. “At the edge of the Universe lies The Utility Room, where all the heavy lifting takes place,” some opening text says. “There have been no work incidents in 13.8 billion years. You are arriving as a tourist. Don’t do anything foolish.” Of course, the first thing you do is break something. The first few levels are admittedly a pretty slow burn. After shattering whatever orb you did in the first couple seconds, you’re teleported to a series of caves with rocks that just sort of slide around and a few jumps over bottomless pits. It is…evocative, I guess. It’s interesting, for instance, that a section consisting solely of pillars and stones would be called “the computer.” Even in these dark and close environments, The Utility Room does a great job of making its world feel, not old per se but more raw. It’s unfinished, because it doesn’t need to be finished. It’s meant to be behind the scenes, messy and coarse compared to our ornate planet. The caves do a decent job of that. But it all feels a bit slight, a bit trivial until you stumble out of the rock and get a true glimpse at what a “behind the scenes” for the universe actually looks like. There’s a grand tradition of putting big, cool stuff in the skybox or background of video games. Space battles, enormous castles, other planets. But more often than not, these are essentially matte paintings, trapped in static loops that keep them unreachably far from the player. What’s so disarming about The Utility Room is its backgrounds seem to operate in this same way, massive objects circling in the distance, but then those objects get closer and closer and reveal themselves to be not background at all but simply so mind-bogglingly huge that their orbits hid their true physicality through layers of cloud and atmosphere. The experience of playing this game, what all the stuff in the caves and over mountain passes and down rocky slides has been leading up to, is this: I walk through a canyon, sides tall, empty. In the center, there’s a broken human face carved from stone. I bend to look at it, only to realize that at the far side of the canyon, something has emerged, moving towards me, and I start to run, but there’s no universe where I can move faster than what’s coming, so as the thing approaches I literally flatten myself against the ground and inches above me, a…head, a head the size of the hoover dam, drifts past without a moment of recognition. I don’t really think The Utility Room is a horror game, or at least it foregoes most of the trappings of the genre. But it does operate on what I’d call “lizard brain fear,” a sense of nope so instinctual there’s no real need to introduce it. Like the environment, the terror this game cultivates is raw. For instance: seeing something big and far away. Making a rough estimate of how big and far away it is, and then realizing that in fact it’s actually much further away then you thought, meaning it’s also much bigger than your original guess, and yet it’s still approaching you at a rate that seems almost impossibly fast for something of that size, and this all happening in VR means you don’t have the luxury of reality-confirming peripheral vision, and something in the deeper parts of your consciousness just goes “get me the f**k out of here.” All this takes place in an aesthetic that reminds me of early 3D renders, compelling planetscapes and horizons completely detached from the expectations of our world. Part of the wonderful eeriness of something like Myst is the tranquility of the background. Those early renders are so deliberately composed and also so still, almost zen-like in the knowledge that the background will never suddenly change, certainly not without input from you. This placidity is also what makes The Utility Room feel so primeval. There is no growing vegetation, no crashing waves or rain or snow. Every environment could have just been sitting there for 13 billion years. Except as soon as you start to feel any sense of zen, a boulder will hurl down a mountain next to you and shatter some of those landmarks that have presumably stood for time immemorial. It’s the feeling of an earthquake caving in Punta Ventana’s natural window, an eruption vivsecting Mount St. Helens. Landscapes are only immutable until met with a greater force. And here, there are great forces. For much of the middle act of The Utility Room, you’re making your way towards a great pendulum in the distance, impossibly long, impossibly thin. Of all the scale you cannot conceptualize in this unfinished world, this is the most extreme. But you inch yourself towards it, through highways of expressionless beings and hand-over-hand up cliff walls, the movement from the pendulum reverberating through the environment even when you cannot see it. And then you find yourself on a thin causeway as the pendulum screams by like a tethered god and the vast heads swim through the air above you and then something else happens: you’re noticed. From the distance appears another Moai and it moves more towards you than one ever has, your incursion in this 13-billion-year-old holding space finally noticed. And perhaps because it is so focused on you it somehow forgets the pendulum. But here, there are great forces. In The Bunker, terror was opening my revolver to see only a single round. Who’s Lila found terror in the movement of an eyebrow, of an upper lip stretched too far. Playing The Utility Room feels instead like watching an asteroid hit the Earth, like watching an alien god die, an event so monumental we don’t have the emotional range to comprehend it. Eldritch, I suppose. But there is no malice here, just uncaring cosmic gears grinding each other to dust. And yet, there are connections between the three. One obvious parallel between them is a loss of control. Your actions in the bunker are limited by the scant supplies it decides to offer, William’s face becomes Lila’s plaything, the movements of the universe go on without you. In a sense, all horror stems from a loss of control. But conversely, what these three titles do, what they must do given their interactive medium, is deny you a total abdication of responsibility. No, if I had a choice, the lights wouldn’t go out. But I did have a choice, I was the one who pushed just a little further into the bunker, I chose to forego fuel to fill my pockets with grenades and bandages. No, William doesn’t have a choice in what Lila does with his body, but even before we were aware of his possession by another entity, we were the ones who puppeteered his face, we were the ones that took away his free will. No one makes us touch those orbs in The Utility Room. Would the head have gotten clobbered if we never showed up? More terrifying than a totally chaotic universe, I think, is the idea of one that presents us with the right choices alongside the wrong ones and we are too naive, or stubborn, or scared, to take them. It’s the idea that agency exists, but only to the extent that we can blame ourselves for what we did wrong. That the path forked miles behind us, but we only realized we took the wrong one once it’s too late to turn back. To give someone control in a video game is to enable them to make mistakes– horror comes from simply living with the consequences.
